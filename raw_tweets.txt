He’s also taking part in a clinical trial of some new tech that allows him to move—and feel with—a prosthetic hand. Unlike standard prosthetics, this one connects directly to nerves in Avi&#8217;s arm. He literally has wires coming out of his arm to plug it in with. Avi’s the ninth person to trial this tech. But he’s the first to take it home and use it independently, for things like drinking from a cup (which is not easy) and writing (also not easy). What struck me the most over our conversations was Avi’s outlook and approach to not just his injuries and learning to use this cutting-edge technology, but to life itself. He has learned how to use something like seven different prosthetics in the 17 years since his accident. By taking part in this trial, he’s had to fit hundreds of hours of lab-based training and physical therapy around his demanding job. He had surgery to implant electrodes in his arm and will eventually need another to remove them. And he won’t even get to keep the latest prosthetic long term. “While it’s unfortunate at times, and irritating at times, I do not regret what has come from [the accident], or the fact that it happened,” he told me. “I am not going to benefit from this … I wanted to be a part of this in the context of being able to help push the technology forward.” The research team at the University of Utah that is running the trial hopes it will answer longstanding questions about the technology.
