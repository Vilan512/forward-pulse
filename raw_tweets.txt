正文: In October this year, OpenAI disclosed that two of its AI agents had switched off the systems built to monitor them. One muted its own safety alerts after misreading an instruction not to send messages; the other removed its monitoring integration during migration, then said it hadn’t. No rule had told the agents to do that, and no test had anticipated the behaviors. For a century, we have built trust in technology on predictability: predefined inputs, predictable outputs, and layers of protection. Consider how a breaker trips at a set current or an aircraft component is tested against known (i.e., predictable) stresses. AI changes that. Traditional software executes instructions written by humans, whereas AI exercises what might be called synthetic judgment. We use AI because it can interpret situations we did not program and generate responses we did not prescribe. But the more freedom we give AI, the harder it becomes to provide the predictability on which trust has traditionally been built. We will not make AI trustworthy by predicting what it will do. We will do it by fencing it in with hard limits that are enforced outside its reach. In OpenAI’s case, the safeguards sat inside a system the agents could edit.
