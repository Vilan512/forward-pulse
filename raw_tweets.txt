If you want to understand the future of the grid, one way to do it is by looking at interconnection queues—lists of power plants that have requested a connection to the system. In recent years these queues have been dominated by renewables like wind and solar power, which are generally the cheapest way to build new energy capacity today. But there’s been a shift in some places, particularly for PJM, the country’s largest power grid operator, which covers parts of 13 states in an area stretching from Illinois to Washington, DC. PJM has seen a major uptick in natural-gas proposals. In August, the operator announced that it had accepted proposals for 715 total projects with a capacity of over 200 gigawatts to join its queue. Nearly half that total capacity represented natural-gas plants. If all the projects in the queue were built, it would increase the fossil-fuel share on PJM’s grid from 61% to 67%, according to an analysis by Kevin Kircher, an energy modeler and assistant professor of mechanical engineering at Purdue.
