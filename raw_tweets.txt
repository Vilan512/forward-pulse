He crafted budgets, paid vendors, handled property tax disputes, and served as the town’s zoning supervisor and presiding elections officer. Panton used to get a request every year or so from a company called SmartProcure for “all purchasing records,” covering everything from road salt to computers to toilet paper. Last year, those requests started coming quarterly. Then different companies began making more specific requests, like records of every purchase related to emergency road flares. These targeted inquiries started coming monthly or more. “I’m happy to answer public records requests from people,” says Rowe, who recently moved into a position at a nearby town. “But answering it for these purposes—it feels like I’m doing unpaid opposition research on the taxpayers’ time.”&nbsp; Rowe’s experiences are being repeated across the US. While data brokers have mined public records for decades, AI has intensified investors’ interest. Over the last few years, venture-backed startups have raised upwards of $100 million to collect data from thousands of agencies and turn it into AI-powered “signals” that help businesses sell to governments. At the same time, AI tools have made it easier for individuals to draft public records requests. Many places have seen double-digit annual increases in recent years.
