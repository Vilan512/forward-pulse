正文: In a research station in central Panama, you can walk through a century of forest time, watching scrubby plots of adolescent trees give way to cathedral-­like canopies of mature growth. Forest ecologist Daisy Dent, a senior scientist at ETH Zurich, oversees this sequence of plots across the Barro Colorado Nature Monument, some of which date as far back as the 19th century. Not one of the trees she surveys was planted by human hands. She maps, measures, and tracks each trunk that emerges, revealing how waves of tree life rise from bare earth.&nbsp; Tropical forests like these are our planet’s biggest terrestrial carbon sink, accounting for around 40% of carbon stored on land. As part of an international network of researchers, Dent is deciphering the networks of animal and plant interactions that enable some forests to recover after destruction. The goal is a low-cost, high-impact alternative to typical carbon-­crediting schemes, which plant huge areas with a single species of tree. For over a decade, her research has built a body of evidence that such large “monocrop” programs could be expensive mistakes—and that more resilient forests can rebound naturally in just a few decades, re-creating ecosystems that suck up vast quantities of carbon dioxide. Few places are better to demonstrate this than Barro Colorado, a reserve covering the forested peninsulas and islands that formed when construction of the Panama Canal flooded the area in 1914. Under the management of the Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute, generations of ecologists have amassed more than a century of records.&nbsp; That means Dent can trace why some plots rebound after clear-cutting for farming and forestry while others falter.
